What This Podcast Is About

The premise. Dr. Jack Logan — a university professor in the social sciences who has spent decades studying comparative religion — argues that the world's religions are not independent inventions. Drawing on scholars like Lord Raglan, René Guénon, Mircea Eliade, Joseph Campbell, and Marinus van der Sluijs, she contends that they descend from a single, pure religious tradition revealed by God to the first human beings at the dawn of history. She calls it the Ancient Tradition.

The method: “stubborn bits.” Like a telephone game played in reverse, she hunts for the motifs that survive intact across continents and millennia — the same creation story, the same divine council, the same dragon battle, the same anointed king — appearing in Egypt, Mesopotamia, Canaan, India, China, Scandinavia, Polynesia, Africa, and the Americas. When a theme can be traced to the world's oldest texts on every continent, she counts it as a fragment of the original revealed theology and pieces it back together, episode by episode, like a college course.

The spirit of it. She insists this is a spiritual pursuit, not just an academic one: the reconstructed theology answers the great questions — Who is God? Why are we here? What happens after death? — and the answers converge into a single coherent, family-centered theology of kingship.

Why a Latter-day Saint will feel at home here

The podcast never mentions The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints or its scriptures. Yet nearly every major conclusion Dr. Logan reaches — by digging through pyramid texts, cuneiform tablets, and Ugaritic epics — lands on ground that is distinctively Restoration doctrine rather than mainstream Christian doctrine: a divine council of gods, a premortal existence, a war in heaven over the Father's throne, creation by organizing eternal matter (not ex nihilo), an embodied Father, a Mother in Heaven, eternal marriage, worlds without number, esoteric temple ordinances required for eternal life, and human beings as literal children of God destined to become kings and queens, priests and priestesses. Her very framing — an original pure religion given to Adam, corrupted over time, recoverable today — is the Latter-day Saint narrative of dispensations, apostasy, and restoration. The Grand Story the Ancients Told

Across 69 episodes, Dr. Logan reconstructs one continuous narrative — “mankind's one great story.” Told in order, it runs from a divine family in eternity, through a war in heaven and the creation, down to you and me and our royal destiny. Every myth she examines is a cultural retelling of some chapter of this story.

Act 1 — Eternity

A divine family reigns in the heavens

Before anything was made, the High God — a glorious, embodied King of righteousness — reigned with his wife, the Queen of Heaven, from a temple-palace at the sacred center of the cosmos. Together they are the Father and Mother of a vast divine family: gods, sons and daughters — including us. The heavens are ordered as a kingdom and governed through a council of the gods, with a high council of twelve princely sons.

Act 2 — Rebellion

The rebel son and the war in heaven (the “primordial theomachy”)

A beloved member of the council rebelled, coveting the Father's throne: Yam refusing homage to El, the Anzu bird stealing the Tablet of Destinies (“I will take kingship for myself”), Set murdering Osiris, Lucifer crying “I will ascend… I will be like the Most High.” The rebel is everywhere depicted as a hideous sea serpent / dragon prowling the chaotic primordial waters. The battle that followed was a spiritual war — fought over the authority of the High God and the order of heaven.

Act 3 — The Champion

The selfless son volunteers and is armed

In the council, a beloved, selfless son — Marduk, Ninurta, Baal, Horus, Christ — volunteers to defend his Father's kingdom. He is armed with a special weapon (Marduk's bow, Excalibur, the sword in the Barnstokkr tree, even ruby slippers) and a protective garment (cloaks, armor, animal skins). Dr. Logan stresses these are symbols: the weapon is the divine word/wind/power of God, given only to the worthy heir, and the garment is divine protection. Before the battle, the gods test his power: he creates and destroys by his word alone.

Act 4 — The Wound

The hero is wounded — and healed by the Tree

The dragon scorches the hero, who crumples under terrible wounds — Osiris is even killed outright. All seems lost. Then comes the healing: the fragrant sap, dew, oil, or honey of the Tree of Life anoints and revives him. Myrrh — the blood-red, bitter, sweet-smelling resin that “bleeds” from its tree — becomes the world's anointing oil. The Christmas tree, the wise men's myrrh, and Mary's spikenard all remember it. The hero's wounds and the tree's balm are the ancient world's picture of sacrifice and atonement.

Act 5 — Coronation

The victor is anointed and crowned King of Kings

Having vanquished the dragon, the son is anointed, enthroned on his Father's throne, and given an inheritance: an eternal kingdom. The Papyrus of Ani says it of Osiris; the Enuma Elish of Marduk; the Baal Cycle of Baal; Revelation 3:21 of Christ — who promises the very same coronation to all who overcome. He is “King of kings” precisely because he makes other kings and queens. And an eternal kingdom cannot be one world: the ancients spoke of innumerable Indras, countless Brahmas, universes beyond universes.

Act 6 — Creation

Order out of chaos: the word, the wind, the light

The pre-creative state was everywhere described as dark, churning primordial waters — a symbol for an infinite ocean of unorganized, eternal matter. Creation ex nihilo is flatly rejected. Creation begins with a thought, then a word (Ptah's Memphite Theology, the Popol Vuh, the Hopi creation song, John 1), carried by a brooding wind — God's breath, his vital essence — which infuses every particle with his light, the energy that counters entropy and holds all things in order. The “Big Bang” is the cosmic egg cracking: God's ineffable light flooding the abyss from the timeless Holy of Holies.

Act 7 — The Sacred Center

The primordial mound, the foundation stone, the cosmic temple

The first creation was a hill rising from the waters — the primordial mound, anchored by the foundation stone where God begins his work and builds his throne and temple. Every pyramid, ziggurat, and sacred mound replicates it; every “center stone” claims it — the Even haShetiyah under the Dome of the Rock, the Kaaba's black stone, the Greek omphalos, Rome's Roma Quadrata, Cusco, the Lia Fáil, Hopi Oraibi. The stone is layered symbolism: where the four corners converge, where God dwells, the Holy of Holies, the “lid of hell” that locks the dragon out — and ultimately a symbol of the Son himself. The whole ordered cosmos is a temple, walled off from the profane waters by a cosmic wall (the ouroboros, the shen ring, the firmament, Marduk's bolt and guard).

Act 8 — Paradise

The earth in heaven's embrace: Eden, the golden age

The newly created earth was not where it is now — it was close to heaven, in an intimate embrace (Sumerian An-Ki, Maori Rangi and Papa, African accounts of the low sky when “God lived among men — with his wife”). That first time — Zep Tepi, Dilmun, the Krita Yuga, the Golden Age, the Dreamtime, the Garden of Eden — knew no death, decay, time, or entropy. Eden itself was the primordial mound: the earth's first temple, where God dwelt, and where (per the Lenape elder Tantaqué's “Turtle Island” account) the sacred tree grew at the center of the North American continent.

Act 9 — Humanity

We arrive: children of the gods, born through the hole between worlds

The ancients say human beings existed before this earth — conscious children of the divine family, begotten in the primordial realm. They came here to gain knowledge of good and evil “to become as the gods” (the Zuni children clamored to come and “grow wiser”). They entered through the sacred center — the sipapu of the Hopi kiva, the Inca place of emergence, a hole between worlds — or were “born from the sacred tree” (Mixtec pochote, Roman oak-born, Sandawe baobab): symbols of birth from divine parents that mark their descendants as royal heirs. Adam and Eve, the first king and queen, were ordained priest and priestess and married by God in the garden temple.

“As above, so below”: revelation, rites, and royal destiny

The goal of it all: that every man and woman conquer the dragon in their own life and be anointed, crowned, and married into the divine family — kings and queens over kingdoms of their own. “Thus the gods did, thus men do.”

Core Themes — the “Stubborn Bits,” Consolidated

Dr. Logan circles the same handful of master themes again and again, adding cultures and texts each pass. Here is each theme stated once, with its strongest evidence, the episodes that develop it, and its Restoration parallel.

1. One original, God-revealed religion

The ancients unanimously claim their religion was revealed, not invented: Thoth revealed every aspect of Egyptian temple worship; Enlil revealed “a mystery of the gods” to Utnapishtim in the ark; Baiame taught initiation rites at the sacred ground of New South Wales; Mahatala gave sacred ceremonies in the Dayak primeval village; God taught Adam and Eve in Eden and revealed “everything from beginning to end” anew to Enoch. The world's religions are the telephone-game descendants of that single revelation — degraded, but with stubborn bits intact.

Ep 1Ep 2Ep 3Ep 4Ep 5
Restoration: the everlasting gospel taught from Adam down through dispensations, lost by apostasy, restored in the latter days (Moses 5:58–59; D&C 128:18). Note 2 Enoch's role here — the Enoch material parallels Moses 6–7, which Joseph Smith produced without access to 2 Enoch (first published in English in 1896).

2. God is knowable

Against Locke, Hume, and Kant (who limited knowledge to the senses) and against Nietzsche, Marx, Durkheim, and Freud (who called religion man-made), the ancient record insists the spiritual realm is knowable by direct experience — and that acquiring spiritual knowledge is necessary to reach one's full potential. Knowledge is the recurring motto of the show.

Ep 2Ep 3
Restoration: “If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God” (James 1:5) — the verse that produced the First Vision; personal revelation (Moroni 10:3–5); “a man is saved no faster than he gets knowledge” (Joseph Smith).

3. The divine council and plurality of gods

Every major ancient culture knew a council of the gods: a Father-King presiding over princely sons who propose, deliberate, vote, and bind themselves by oath; a high council of twelve; the council as a court of judges (echoed today in a judge presiding over a twelve-person jury). The Hebrew Bible preserves it (Psalm 82: “God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods”), and John 1 places Christ in that primordial council as a God assigned to create. The council chose the champion who would face the dragon, and the rebellion of one of its members began the war.

Restoration: the Grand Council (Abraham 3:22–28); “the Gods” of the Abrahamic creation account (Abraham 4); the Twelve Apostles enthroned as judges (Luke 22:29–30; D&C 29:12); the King Follett discourse on the plurality of gods.

4. Creation from eternal matter — never from nothing

The primordial waters symbolize an infinite, chaotic, pre-existing substance with the potential for life. Creation is the imposing of order on it — sanctifying profane elements into sacred creation. Dr. Logan repeatedly notes this matches modern physics (conservation of energy, E=mc²) and that the ancients beat astrophysicists to the multiverse, the Big Bang singularity, and timelessness “before” time.

Restoration: “The elements are eternal” (D&C 93:33); the Gods “organized and formed” the heavens and earth (Abraham 4:1); Joseph Smith: creation from chaos, “element had an existence from the time He had” (King Follett discourse).

5. The word, the wind, and the light

Creation proceeds: divine thought → divine word (Ptah creates by heart and tongue; “Let there be light”; the Popol Vuh's word; the Hopi creation song) → the brooding wind, God's breath/vital essence, which infuses every element with his light — the energy that counters the second law of thermodynamics, holds planets in orbit, and transmits truth (her fiber-optics analogy). “Ptah is in all things” = God's sustaining light is in all things; that is why winds and seas obey Christ.

Restoration: D&C 88:6–13 is nearly a restatement — the Light of Christ “which is in all things, which giveth life to all things, which is the law by which all things are governed”; “the word of my power” (Moses 1:32).

6. The dragon and the war in heaven

The rebel of the council is everywhere a sea serpent / dragon of the chaotic deep: Tiamat, Yam, Leviathan, Rahab, Apophis, Anzu, Vritra, Kaliya, Fafnir, Set (as usurping murderer), Lucifer (“I will ascend above the heights… I will be like the Most High”). His rebellion is the refusal to honor the High God and the cosmic law; his weapons are chaos, spiritual wounds, and death. Good appears to lose (Osiris dies!) before the healing and victory. Even fairy tales keep the plot: every hero from Sigurd to Luke Skywalker, Cinderella to Katniss, is retelling the theomachy.

Restoration: the war in heaven over agency and the Father's throne (Moses 4:1–4; Revelation 12); “that old serpent, who is the devil” (D&C 76:28; 2 Nephi 2:18); the battle continues in each soul.

7. The anointing — becoming a “christ”

The coronation series (occasioned by Charles III's 2023 coronation) uncovers the world's oldest rite: the king anointed with myrrh oil — the gift and sap of the Tree of Life — making him a sacral king “after the order of Melchizedek” (melek = king + tzedek = righteousness). Egyptian texts put the ointment of Horus's forehead on king Unas to make him divine (“akh-ified” — become like God). To be a Christian is literally to claim to be anointed as Christ was anointed; branches of Christianity that kept the anointing rite stand closer to the original tradition.

8. Kingship is the heart of it all

God is a heavenly King (Egyptian nswt, Akkadian malku, Hebrew melek) reigning from a throne in a temple-palace at the cosmic center — attested by Egyptians, Mesopotamians, Canaanites, Hebrews, Persians (Ahura Mazda), Hindus (Indra), Chinese (the Jade Emperor), Greeks, Norse, Aztecs, Incas, Hawaiians… Kingship as an institution appears fully formed with the first written records, and earthly coronations are replicas of the heavenly enthronement. The destiny of the faithful is not merely salvation but coronation: “He that overcometh, I will grant to sit with me in my throne” — the Sumerian temple hymn even says the holy mountain “gives birth to kings, plural.”

9. The sacred center — stone, mound, and temple

Everything radiates from the sacred center: the foundation stone, the primordial mound, the temple, the throne, the tree. Pyramids and ziggurats on five continents are stylized primordial mounds; cities, temples, and entire nations are founded on “center stones” (Jerusalem's Even haShetiyah, the Kaaba, Delphi's omphalos, Roma Quadrata, Cusco's wanka, Ireland's Lia Fáil, Hopi Oraibi). Its symbols: the circumpunct ⊙, the bindu, the swastika (blessings flowing from the center to the four corners), the royal umbrella, the four-pillared canopy. Rituals enact it: circumambulating the Kaaba, ring dances, wedding circuits. The center is where heaven touches earth — and the stone, layer upon layer, is finally a symbol of Christ himself, the “lid of hell” that locks the dragon down.

10. God is embodied, sexed, and married

The “Who is God?” series argues from the oldest texts that God is (a) corporeal — a glorified body of flesh and bone (the risen Christ eats fish and honeycomb precisely to prove it); (b) sexed — gods are male or female, each half of a reproductive whole (the bodiless, sexless God enters Judaism and Christianity late, via Plato through Philo and Maimonides); and (c) married — An & Ki, Atum & Iusaas, El & Athirat (Asherah), Zeus & Hera, Odin & Frigg (“Beloved”), Izanagi & Izanami. The Queen of Heaven is the “progenitress of the gods,” symbolized by the sacred tree; her Ugaritic epithet is qudšu — “Holiness” itself. Even Mary Magdalene's epithet, “the Tower,” marks a holy woman as a personified sanctuary.

11. The divine family — and our premortal place in it

The deep structure of every ancient pantheon is not a committee but a family: Father, Mother, and their sons and daughters. El is “father of gods and men”; gods and humans are the same species at different stages of development and sanctification. Cosmic-egg myths, Zuni and Hopi emergence accounts, and Eden itself attest that human beings lived as conscious children in the primordial realm and chose to come to a fallen world to learn good from evil and “become as the gods.”

12. Marriage at the center — the crowning ordinance

God married Adam and Eve in the garden temple. The Hindu wedding seats bride and groom — ritually a queen and king, anointed with haldi (turmeric) — beneath the mandap, a four-pillared canopy over the fire altar marking the Holy of Holies; the Jewish chuppah and the church ciborium/baldachin are the same structure. Couples circumambulate the altar or each other (hakafot, the hora; Izanagi and Izanami circling the heavenly pillar). Royal marriage at the sacred center — Rama and Sita, Siddhartha crowned with his wife and given “the whole kingdom” — fuses anointing, kingship, queenship, and the center into one rite. The Holy of Holies was anciently associated with the bridal chamber.

13. Esoteric knowledge and initiation

Mesopotamia's seven sages (apkallu), “perfect in lofty wisdom,” sent by Enki, taught an esoteric “secret of the gods” reserved for the fully initiated and essential to eternal life — including rites that sanctify the body (Marduk: “Where are the seven sages of the Apsu who can make my body holy?”). Utnapishtim's rite in the ark conferred eternal life. Zoroastrianism's seven Amesha Spentas, Egypt's temple mysteries, Hopi ceremonies from Maasaw — the original religion had an inner, ritual, covenant core, not just public preaching. Modern religion that is purely exoteric has lost something the ancients considered indispensable.

14. “As above, so below”

The governing principle of the ancient world: earth replicates heaven. Temples model the cosmos; coronations model the Son's enthronement; weddings model the divine marriage; the ring dance models the circle around the throne; the king is God's image and stand-in (Rome's rex as Jupiter's earthly representative). “Thus the gods did, thus men do” (Satapatha Brahmana). We are to imitate the hero's preparations — acquire his weapon (the word), his garment (protection), his anointing — because his story is the script for ours.

15. Ma'at / Tzedek — the cosmic law of righteousness

Beneath everything is one law: Egyptian ma'at, Hebrew tzedek, Vedic ṛta, Zoroastrian asha — truth, justice, rightness, cosmic order. God is God because he perfectly upholds it; his power flows from his righteousness; the dragon is its antithesis (chaos). Creation, kingship, temple, and salvation all run on this law — Dr. Logan playfully calls it the real “Theory of Everything.” God doesn't merely do righteousness; he loves it — so he can be trusted absolutely.

A note on the Father / Son distinction

The ancient pattern consistently shows a High God (El, An/Anu, Atum) who presides, and a beloved son (Baal, Marduk/Ninurta, Osiris/Horus) who fights the dragon, creates, is crowned, and administers the kingdom. Dr. Logan reads John 1, Psalm 2, Psalm 110, and Hebrews 1 the same way: the Father enthrones the Son, who is the active creator-king — “the cosmic Christ,” the carpenter (Greek tektōn, master builder) of a cosmic temple. This two-figure structure is awkward for strict trinitarianism but is precisely the Latter-day Saint understanding: Elohim the Father, and Jehovah/Christ the Son — the God of the Old Testament, creator under the Father's direction.

Key Texts, Scholars & Name Corrections

Ancient texts she returns to constantly

Enuma Elish — Babylonian creation epic (Marduk vs. Tiamat); tablets from Ashurbanipal's library, found 1849.

Pyramid Texts (kings Unas, Pepi II; esp. PT 600) — oldest religious corpus in Egypt; the primordial mound, anointing.

Papyrus of Ani (Egyptian Book of the Dead) — Osiris enthroned as heavenly king on his father's throne.

Memphite Theology / Shabaka Stone — Ptah creates by thought and word; the green breccia stone later reused as a millstone (British Museum).

Bremner-Rhind Papyrus — Egyptian creation and Apophis texts.

Epic of Gilgamesh — Utnapishtim's flood account and the secret rite of the gods in the ark.

Anzu myth — the bird-monster steals the Tablet of Destinies; Ninurta defeats him.

Kesh Temple Hymn — possibly the world's oldest religious text (~2600 BC), from Nippur.

Enki and the World Order; Sumerian King List — kingship lowered from heaven; the mountain “giving birth to kings.”

Ugaritic Baal Cycle (Ras Shamra tablets) — El, Athirat, Baal vs. Yam; “eternal kingdom, everlasting dominion.”

2 Enoch / Book of the Holy Secrets of Enoch — her favorite pseudepigraphon: Enoch's ascent, Adam crowned king, ADAM four-directions anagram.

Joseph and Aseneth — pseudepigraphon: the glorious heavenly man, the honeycomb, Aseneth's transformation into a radiant being.

Hebrew Bible — Genesis 1–3 (Eden as sanctuary — Richard Davidson's “Earth's First Sanctuary”), Psalms (82; 89; 110; royal/enthronement psalms), Isaiah 6, 14, 27, 51; Job 38, 41.

New Testament — John 1; Matthew 2 (Magi), 16:18; Luke 22:29–30, 24:36–48; Hebrews 1–2; Revelation 1, 3:21, 12, 19.

Avesta / Gathas & Yashts — Ahura Mazda and the seven Amesha Spentas.

Rig Veda; Satapatha Brahmana; Bhagavata Purana; Brahmavaivarta Purana (parade of Indras); Ramayana — Indra/Vritra; Krishna/Kaliya; Rama and Sita's wedding.

Popol Vuh — Maya creation by the word (the manuscript from Santo Tomás Chichicastenango).

Kojiki — Izanagi and Izanami circle the heavenly pillar.

Talmud & Zohar — Leviathan's skin made into a tabernacle; the stone cast into the abyss.
